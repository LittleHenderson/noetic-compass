# Quick-Start: If You Only Have One Hour

Some readers will not start at Chapter 1. You have a specific problem, you
bought this book because the description sounded like it addressed that problem,
and you want to know within the hour whether it does.

Fair. Here is the shortest honest path.

---

## The five minutes that matter most

Everything in this book rests on one claim, and if you reject it the rest will
not land:

> **A bias controls the path from a cause to an effect the same way a base
> current controls collector-to-emitter flow.**

That is the Cognitive Junction Transistor. Three terminals. A cause arrives, a
bias decides how much of it gets through, an effect leaves. Change the bias and
the same cause produces a different effect — not because the cause changed, but
because the thing standing between them did.

Almost everything people call willpower, discipline, conditioning, framing, or
persuasion is an operation on somebody's bias terminal. Once you can see the
terminal, you can see who is reaching for it.

If that sentence did something for you, read on. If it sounds like a metaphor
being oversold, Chapter 3 is where the claim gets made properly, and it is the
chapter to judge the book by.

## The one-hour route

Read these four things, in this order. Skip everything else for now.

**1. Chapter 2, sections 2.1 through 2.3** *(about 20 minutes)*
The notation. You need the ten Noetics, the four Domains, and how to read a
state literal. Do not try to memorise the whole system — read `^3^P_{F7}|T` as
"principled aversion, purpose-driven, in the transcendent register" and move on.
The rest of the notation will come when you need it.

**2. Chapter 3, sections 3.2 and 3.3** *(about 15 minutes)*
The three terminals and the six parameters. Pay attention to **β** (gain) and
**sat** (the ceiling). Those two do most of the work in most boards.

**3. Chapter 12** *(about 10 minutes)*
How to read a schematic. Short, and without it the board chapters are pictures
rather than drawings.

**4. Whichever board addresses your actual problem** *(about 15 minutes)*
Read its requirements, its block diagram, and its worked example. Skip the CCDL
listing on first pass — come back to it.

## Read this before you run anything

Three things will save you the most common failures, and they cost you a
paragraph each.

**Gate the operation before you build it.** Chapter 21 is the prerequisite gate:
is this actually what you want, do you have the drive, do you have the
schematic, can you execute. Most operations that fail did not fail in
execution — they failed authentication, and the operator ran them anyway.

**Pick a saturation ceiling deliberately, and pick it low.** This is the most
counterintuitive result in the book and it appears in every board. A stage that
saturates *early* is a stage that has stopped responding to its input, which is
exactly what you want from a commitment. A high ceiling leaves headroom for a
sufficiently forceful input to keep pushing the decision around. Chapter 10 does
this properly.

**You cannot un-cast.** Chapter 21 closes on section-retraction, and the
practical content is this: prerequisite satisfaction runs one way. Having
satisfied a prerequisite, you cannot return to the state of not having satisfied
it. Operations are not reversible just because you changed your mind about them.
That is the strongest argument in this book for reading Chapter 24 before you
run anything ambitious.

## What this book will not do for you

It will not make you effective at things you have not practised. St. John's
Equation, in Chapter 5, is in large part an argument about how much time
competence takes; nothing in the framework shortens that.

It will not suspend causality. A desire board organises attention, effort, and
follow-through — which is a great deal, and is why the boards work at all — but
it does not act at a distance on the world, and the book never claims it does.

It will not diagnose or treat anything. If you are unwell, see a clinician.

And it will not tell you what to want. The circuit is neutral about what runs
through it; a transistor amplifies whatever arrives at its cause terminal. The
engineering belongs to everyone. The ethics belong to you.
