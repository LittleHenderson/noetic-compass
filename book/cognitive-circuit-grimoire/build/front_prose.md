## A Note on Ethics

This book is **neutral** on what you build. A CJT amplifies whatever you feed its cause terminal — a healing intention or a curse, a liberation narrative or a control program. The circuit doesn't care. The resistor limits flow; it doesn't ask whether the flow is benevolent.

The Cognitive Safety Board (Chapter 24) is the minimum viable safety architecture. It is not optional. Every board in this book includes a CSB stage. An operator who removes the safety stage from their circuit has made an ethical choice — and the engineering consequences of that choice (inversion, runaway oscillation, dielectric breakdown, fuse failure at the wrong terminal) are predictable and severe.

The author's position is this: the engineering belongs to everyone. The ethics belong to the operator. And an operator who cannot articulate WHY they built their circuit the way they did — who cannot trace the signal path from cause to effect, who cannot name their bias terminal, who cannot measure their constraint resistance — is not an operator. They are a component in someone else's circuit.

# How to Use This Grimoire

This is a bench manual. It is meant to sit open beside the work, spine cracked,
with your own numbers written in the margins. It is not meant to be read once
and shelved.

That has consequences for how it is built, and knowing them will save you time.

---

## The book has four parts and they are not equally urgent

**Part I — Foundations** is the notation and the physics. Five chapters. You
cannot skip these. Everything after them is written in a language taught here,
and a reader who skips to the board catalog will find themselves looking at
`^3^P_{F7}|T` and guessing. Guessing produces circuits that do not work and,
worse, circuits that appear to work.

**Part II — The Component Library** is the parts bin. Six chapters, one per
component class, each with a datasheet. You do not have to read Part II
straight through. You do have to read Chapter 10 before you design anything,
because the Cognitive Junction Transistor is in every board in this book and
selecting its parameters badly is the single most common way an operation
fails.

**Part III — The Board Catalog** is nine complete boards. Read Chapter 12
first — it teaches you to read a schematic — then go to whichever board you
need. The chapters are deliberately independent. You can read Chapter 20 on
self-programming without having read Chapter 16 on conditioning, and most
readers should.

**Part IV — Operations** is what you do with a board once you have one:
gating it, measuring it, calibrating it, and refusing to run it. Chapter 24,
the Cognitive Safety Board, is the last chapter because it is the one that has
to hold everything before it. It is not optional and it is not an appendix.

## On the parameters

**Every number in this book is a design choice, not a measurement.** When
Chapter 13 says the amplification stage runs at β=2.4, that is a specified
value with a stated reason and a stated failure mode at other values. It is not
a reading taken off an instrument, and the book never pretends otherwise.

This matters because the alternative — presenting chosen values as measured
ones — is how a framework becomes unfalsifiable. Where something *has* been
measured, the book says what was measured and how. Where it has not, it says
that too.

## The five minutes that matter most

Everything in this book rests on one claim, and if you reject it the rest will
not land:

> **A bias controls the path from a cause to an effect the same way a base
> current controls collector-to-emitter flow.**

That is the Cognitive Junction Transistor. Three terminals. A cause arrives, a
bias decides how much of it gets through, an effect leaves. Change the bias and
the same cause produces a different effect — not because the cause changed, but
because the thing standing between them did.

Almost everything people call willpower, discipline, conditioning, framing, or
persuasion is an operation on somebody's bias terminal. Once you can see the
terminal, you can see who is reaching for it.

If that sentence did something for you, read on. If it sounds like a metaphor
being oversold, Chapter 3 is where the claim gets made properly, and it is the
chapter to judge the book by.

## Read this before you run anything

Three things will save you the most common failures, and they cost you a
paragraph each.

**Gate the operation before you build it.** Chapter 21 is the prerequisite gate:
is this actually what you want, do you have the drive, do you have the
schematic, can you execute. Most operations that fail did not fail in
execution — they failed authentication, and the operator ran them anyway.

**Pick a saturation ceiling deliberately, and pick it low.** This is the most
counterintuitive result in the book and it appears in every board. A stage that
saturates *early* is a stage that has stopped responding to its input, which is
exactly what you want from a commitment. A high ceiling leaves headroom for a
sufficiently forceful input to keep pushing the decision around. Chapter 10 does
this properly.

**You cannot un-cast.** Chapter 21 closes on section-retraction, and the
practical content is this: prerequisite satisfaction runs one way. Having
satisfied a prerequisite, you cannot return to the state of not having satisfied
it. Operations are not reversible just because you changed your mind about them.
That is the strongest argument in this book for reading Chapter 24 before you
run anything ambitious.

It will not make you effective at things you have not practised. St. John's

it does not act at a distance on the world, and the book never claims it does.

through it; a transistor amplifies whatever arrives at its cause terminal. The
engineering belongs to everyone. The ethics belong to you.
